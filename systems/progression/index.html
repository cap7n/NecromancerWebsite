    Progression & The Campaign Arc¶

    The arc was stated 2026-07-26

    The shape below came from the designer. It is the spine the rest of the game hangs on, and it changes the early game considerably: you do not start with a horde.

    The arc¶

    Act 1: the basement¶

    One skeleton. You raise it in the cellar of some village, and it is pathetic. You are hiding. A single militiaman would kill you.

    Verbs at this stage are small and quiet: rob a grave, poison a well, drag a body somewhere private. Corpses arrive one at a time and each one matters. Nothing here resembles commanding an army.

    Act 2: the scavenger¶

    A dozen. You roam looking for other people's battles and arrive afterwards to harvest what is left. You poison villages to raise mortality and come back later for the results. Open fights are still lost fights.

    This is where the economy teaches itself: corpses are scarce, so the parasitic loop is felt rather than explained.

    Act 3: the first holding¶

    You take a village. Everything changes, because now you have a place:

    • Undead become a workforce. They dig, haul, and build. This is the point at which the horde stops being purely military. See labour below.
    • Rituals become possible, because a ritual needs somewhere to perform it.
    • Your first lieutenant is summoned, at great cost. See origin.

    Act 4: the warlord¶

    Regiments, detachments, open battle. The horde becomes an army, heat rises, and coalitions form against you. This is the part the rest of the wiki has been describing, and it is the last act rather than the starting position.

    The arc is also the tutorial

    Nothing here needs a tutorial layer bolted on. You learn the corpse economy when corpses are scarce, aura control when you have five units and can see every one of them, and detachments only once you own a lieutenant. Each system arrives when it is the only thing you have to think about.

    It also quietly answers Q14: the player experiences 1, then 10, then 50, then 800 in sequence, so "what reads as a horde" gets calibrated by playing rather than by us guessing.

    Lieutenants are summoned, not promoted¶

    An earlier proposal had them raised from named enemies you had personally killed. Rejected in favour of ritual summoning at great effort, which fits the arc far better:

    • A ritual needs a place, so lieutenants are gated behind holding territory rather than behind combat luck.
    • "A lot of effort" is a real cost gate that the player feels, and it makes the first one an event.
    • It ties your tactical ceiling to your strategic progress, which is the connection the campaign layer needs.

    Players name their own. Cheap to build, and it does real work: a named Overseer you summoned at ruinous cost is something you will actually mind losing, which is exactly the "something to lose" role they exist to fill.

    Labour: the horde works¶

    New mechanic, stated 2026-07-26. Once you hold a settlement, undead are a workforce as well as an army. They dig, haul, build, and power rituals.

    This matters more than it first sounds, because it puts your one resource under genuine tension:

    Every skeleton digging is a skeleton not fighting.

    Army size and economic output come out of the same pool. That is a real strategic dial that costs nothing extra to build, since the units already exist, and it gives the horde a purpose between battles instead of standing around being upkeep.

    OPEN

    How granular? A slider between labour and war, assigned regiments, or per-building garrisons? Recommend: assign whole regiments to work, so it reuses the regiment system rather than adding a second one.

    The three progression tracks¶

    Track Currency Feel
    The Necromancer Anima Rare, permanent, chosen. A handful of big decisions per campaign, not a skill grid.
    The Horde Corpses, Reagents, recipes Unlocks kinds of unit and better raising, not stat bumps.
    The Domain Territory, necropolis buildings, labour Where the long arc lives, and what gates lieutenants.

    Keeping them separate means a player can be strong in one and weak in another, which is what makes two co-op players non-interchangeable.

    Rules of thumb¶

    • No level-up stat inflation. Undead should not get numerically better; the player should get new options. Numbers going up fights Pillar 2 by making individual units precious.
    • Anima spends are irreversible. Rare currency plus permanent choice equals a build.
    • The best upgrades extend control, not damage. More Will, wider aura, another lieutenant. Those change how combat plays rather than how fast it ends.

    Open¶

    • Does progress persist across campaigns, or is each self-contained?
    • In co-op, is progression per-player or partly shared?
    • Is there an end condition, or is it a sandbox?
